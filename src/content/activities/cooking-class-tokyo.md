Tokyo cooking classes range widely in what they actually teach — sushi rolling, ramen broth and noodle-making,
izakaya-style small dishes, and full multi-course meals are all commonly offered, often at similar price
points, with quite different levels of hands-on involvement.

## What varies between class types

- **Sushi classes** are often more demonstration-heavy with a shorter hands-on rolling portion, since proper
  sushi rice preparation takes time that most short classes skip.
- **Ramen classes** tend to be more hands-on with noodle-making or broth assembly, since the process lends
  itself better to a shorter guided format.
- **Izakaya/multi-dish classes** usually involve preparing several smaller dishes, giving more variety but less
  depth on any single technique.

## What to check before booking

- **How hands-on the class actually is** — some listings are closer to a cooking demonstration with tasting
  than genuine hands-on instruction; check reviews for specifics.
- **Whether a market visit is included** — classes that start with a trip to a local market for ingredients run
  longer and offer more context, but cost more and take a bigger chunk of your day.
- **Group size and English instruction quality** — smaller classes generally mean more individual guidance,
  which matters more for genuinely learning a technique versus just watching one.
- **Dietary restrictions** — flag allergies or dietary needs when booking rather than day-of, since ingredients
  are often pre-purchased for the group.

## Bottom line

Decide whether you want to genuinely learn a technique (favor smaller, hands-on classes) or want a broader food
experience with some cooking involved (market-tour combos or multi-dish izakaya classes), then check reviews
for how hands-on the specific listing actually is before booking.
